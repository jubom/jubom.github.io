The readiness gap

Enterprises are deploying AI faster than they can trust their data.

The symptoms show up in the boardroom, not the data center. Every one of them traces back to data nobody has certified, protected, or defined.

Two answers to one question

The AI assistant and the executive dashboard report different revenue. Once leaders see that, trust in both collapses, and adoption stalls.

Exposure no one can see

Personal and regulated data flows into AI pipelines untagged and unmasked. The risk is real long before anyone can measure it.

Pilots that never ship

Proofs of concept succeed in a sandbox, then stall at production because no one can say which data is fit for AI to use.

Perspective

Four principles I bring to every AI readiness program

AI governance is not a separate discipline. It is data governance carried through to the moment a person or a machine acts on the data.

  • AI readiness is a certification, not a feeling.

    If no one can name the owner, the definition, the quality threshold, and the known limits, the data isn't ready for AI, however good it looks.

  • Built is not the same as certified.

    Implementation teams build. Governance decides what the business may rely on. Keeping those roles separate is what makes trust auditable.

  • Find it before you protect it.

    Masking, access control, and AI guardrails all depend on knowing where sensitive data lives. Classification comes first, or every later control is guesswork.

  • Measure trust and adoption, not artifacts.

    The number of policies written means little. What matters is how much of the business runs on certified data, and how often AI gets it wrong.

Approach

From ungoverned data to AI the business can trust

  1. Assess exposure

    Quantify where sensitive and business-critical data lives, and what is ungoverned today.

  2. Govern meaning

    Assign owners, define metrics once, and set the quality bar each domain must meet.

  3. Certify for AI

    Apply explicit AI-readiness criteria before any dataset or metric reaches an agent or copilot.

  4. Enable and monitor

    Teach people what to trust and why, then track adoption, drift, and recertification.

Case studies

Business problems, governed

Privacy and risk

Making invisible risk visible, then fixable

The business problem
Sensitive data was spreading into analytics and AI tools with almost no controls. Under 1% of ~1,600 sensitive columns were tagged, and none were masked.
What I did
Quantified exposure by risk tier, set the tagging standard, and designed a phased program: a 7-table pilot, full high-risk coverage in 8 weeks, then masking and row-level security.
Why it matters
An unmeasured liability became a sequenced decision a steering committee could act on.

AI readiness

One definition of the business, for people and AI

The business problem
Dashboards, natural-language analytics, and AI agents each calculated the same metrics differently, so the answer depended on which tool you asked.
What I did
Designed a 6-stage certification lifecycle with explicit AI-readiness criteria, and an operating model separating the partner who builds the semantic layer from the team that certifies it.
Why it matters
Executives and AI systems get the same answer, and someone is accountable for it.

Vendor evaluation

Buying AI tools on evidence, not demos

The business problem
An AI-assisted data quality vendor proposed a proof of concept built to show its strengths, not to test it against how the business actually operates.
What I did
Mapped 17 capabilities and 19 rule candidates against internal success criteria, added the missing tests, and verified data availability before kickoff.
Why it matters
A platform decision grounded in the operating model the business will live with for years.
